Nothing in your folder stands alone

Every page should answer a question raised by the page before it.

  2. which raises the question: Who is this really for?

  4. which raises the question: What do their answers mean?

If a page doesn’t answer a question…

If it doesn’t lead anywhere…

…ask what question it should now be raising for the next page.

01

Part 1

Understanding the Problem

Task analysis · narrowing down · consumer profile · existing products

Stage 1

Mind Map 1 — Task Analysis

What it contains

Take the context AQA has given you and branch out — every problem, activity, user group and friction you can find inside it. Breadth first, judgement later.

Why it’s done

Moderators specifically mark down folders that jump to one problem without showing a range was considered. This map is your evidence that you looked before you chose.

What a good branch looks like

  • A named friction or problem — not a vague topic
  • Who experiences it, and when
  • Why it currently isn’t solved well
  • A rough sense of how big / worth solving it is

Weak or strong branch?

Weak version

“I could design packaging, or a toy, or a chair.”

Strong version

“Parents packing school lunches struggle to keep hot and cold food separate and safe — existing boxes don’t manage temperature at all.”

From a real GCSE folder

  • Task analysis. One word — chair — branched into needs, who, materials and lifestyles before any brief was written.

Stage 2

Mind Map 2 — Narrowing Down

What it contains

Take the one friction, activity or problem you’ve selected from Mind Map 1 and branch again — this time on that single idea. What causes it? Who does it affect most? What would ‘solved’ even look like?

Why it’s done

This is where a vague topic becomes a real design problem. It’s also your justification for the choice — the moderator should see why this friction, not the others, deserved a full NEA.

From Mind Map 1 → Mind Map 2

Mind Map 1: “Lunch packaging”

  • Heat loss
  • Mess
  • Portion control
  • Bag weight
  • Bin waste
  • Allergy labelling…

Mind Map 2: “Heat loss”

  • Insulation methods
  • How long food stays warm
  • What parents currently do about it
  • What a fix would need to fit into a school bag

One line here should be a direct answer to a question the first map raised.

From a real GCSE folder

  • Narrowing down. Every room, product, design movement and target market is given a reason to keep it or drop it. (A different project.)

Your choice

From here, two honest starting points

Once your focus is set, how you begin is up to you — play to how you think best. Both paths are equally valid; both must show evidence.

Sketch it out first

A mind-dump of first-thought ideas — rough, quick, unfiltered. Good if ideas come easily to you and you think best with a pencil in hand.

Still needs labelling and dating — these count as your first concept sketches.

Research it first

Go deeper into the friction before sketching anything — consumer profile, existing products. Good if you want a firmer footing before committing ideas to paper.

Sketches will follow research rather than lead it — both are fine.

Stage 3

Consumer Profile — values, not answers

What it contains

A real client or user, questioned about the friction you’ve chosen — their habits, priorities, constraints and preferences around it.

Why it’s done

It grounds the friction in a real person before you design anything, and it hands you the language you’ll justify every later specification point with.

The trap to avoid

Don’t ask “what’s your favourite colour?” — that hands the pupil an answer and quietly closes down creativity. Ask what they value and why instead.

Answers vs. values & preferences

  • “What colour do you want?” “Do you prefer things that blend in or stand out?”
  • “Do you want it made of wood?” “Does it matter to you where materials come from?”
  • “What size should it be?” “How much space are you working with day to day?”

Stage 3, continued

Who they are is weak. How they live is useful.

Weak

“My consumer is a teenage girl who supports LFC.”

A label, not a person. It tells you nothing about what to design.

Strong

“A 14-year-old student with a small bedroom, limited floor space, and a lot of school equipment to store.”

Tells you what to design for: compact, lightweight, easy to tidy away.

From a real GCSE folder

  • Values, not answers. Twelve poles-apart scales, then each answer recorded with the consumer’s reason.

  • From answers to insights. Each response is turned into what it means for the chair — size, motion, materials, storage.

Stage 4

Existing Products — finding the gaps

What it contains

Products that already try to solve this friction — tested, used, or taken apart, not just looked at.

The one question that matters

Not “what does this product do?” but “where does it fall short of my consumer’s needs — and is that gap worth designing for?”

Feeds directly into your specification

  • What this product gets right — keep that
  • What it misses for your specific consumer
  • Why that gap still exists (cost? complexity? nobody asked?)

Each gap you find here should turn up later as a specification point, with this analysis cited as the reason it’s there.

Example page format

  • Judge each product on its own, then pull them together. Each product gets its own evaluation — what it gets right, what it misses — before a final paragraph says what the gap across all three actually is, and what that means for the specification. Replace the placeholder images with photos of the real products you’ve found.

If your product analysis doesn’t change or confirm anything in your thinking, it’s an activity you did for its own sake — and it won’t be credited.

Part 2

Setting the Target

The design brief and specification

Stage 5

What makes a strong specification point

Measurable

A number, a material, a limit — something you can actually test against later, not a feeling.

Justified

You can say exactly why this point exists and what it’s protecting.

Traceable to the profile

Linked to something your consumer actually said they need or value.

Traceable to research

Linked to a gap you found in existing products, not invented from nowhere.

Every point needs a visible trail back

Weak — no traceable origin

“The product should be lightweight.”

Strong — cause named, evidence named

“The prototype must weigh under 600g, so a 14-year-old can carry it in a school bag alongside books all day.”
Consumer profile
Carries a full bag between lessons, already complains about weight
Existing products
The two competitor products tested both weighed over 900g

From a real GCSE folder

  • The design brief. Look at the bottom of the page: who the chair is for, where it will be used and what it needs to do — the brief every specification point has to serve.

  • A visible trail back. Measurable criteria (800 × 800mm footprint, 40mm padding, 100kg load), each justified by the consumer research.

Stage 5, continued

The specification stays alive, not fixed

It isn’t a one-time gate

The NEA isn’t a straight line. If research or testing later on reveals something the spec should cover, add it — that’s the process working, not going backwards.

Show the update, don’t hide it

Use a dated note or a different text colour when a point is added or changed. It’s evidence of you responding to evidence — exactly what examiners want to see.

Simple methods that work well

  1. Joints must withstand repeated flexing without failure. Added 14 Nov — after testing showed my first joint cracked under load.

Part 3 · The heart of the NEA

The Iterative Loop

Idea → evaluate → question → research → develop → next idea

Runs throughout

This loop runs from your first idea to your last

Click each step — or use the arrow keys — to go round the loop.

Round again, until the design is resolved

Step 1 of 5

Idea

An idea goes on the page — labelled and dated, so it counts as evidence. It might come from your first mind-dump of sketches, or from the last time round this loop.

Step 2 of 5

Evaluate against spec

Check the idea against your specification — point by point, not a general impression. Where it falls short, name the exact spec point it fails.

Step 3 of 5

Question: “How can I…?”

The question is the hinge. “How do I join these?” or “How do I curve this?” turns a stuck point into a targeted piece of research.

Step 4 of 5

Research

Research aimed exactly at the question the last idea raised — not a topic in general — which then gets tested.

Step 5 of 5

Develop / next idea

What you tested gets sketched into the next idea — and a strong point from it can update your specification at any stage.

The loop, continued

The loop in action — a real example

Before each reveal, ask: what should happen next?

Step 1 of 5

  1. Idea 3

    A curved backrest sketched for comfort and looks.

  2. Evaluate

    Doesn’t meet spec point 4 — must be made from solid timber.

  3. Question

    “How can I curve solid wood into this shape?”

  4. Research

    Investigates steam bending and laminating; tests a small sample of each.

  5. Idea 4

    Redraws the backrest using the laminating method that held its shape best.

The same loop in a real GCSE folder

  • Idea. A first idea modelled in birch plywood and annotated against what the consumer wanted.

  • Evaluate and question. Each CAD development is evaluated, and ends with a “How can I…?” question in purple.

  • Research, then develop. “How will I make the curved structure?” leads to laminating, rib slotting and layering — each drawn back onto the chair.

The habit most folders are missing

Explain what you left behind, too

Evaluating why an idea was developed is only half the judgement. The other half — just as heavily rewarded — is explaining why an idea wasn’t.

Usually done well

“I chose idea 3 because it best matches spec points 2, 5 and 7…”

Often missing, or just “I didn’t like this one”

Not enough “I didn’t like this one.”

“Idea 1 was rejected because it failed spec point 4 (under 600g) — the folded-steel frame alone weighed 800g, and reinforcing it would only add more.”

From a real GCSE folder

  • Ideas left behind. Look for the rejections: a seat that “resembles a toilet seat”, and a frame that “steps too far away from the original design”.

Stage 6

Bring the client back in during development

What it contains

Show your developed ideas to your client and ask which they’d choose, and why — not just whether they like them.

Why it’s done

A folder full of only your own judgement is missing half the evidence AQA is looking for. Ongoing third-party feedback — not one meeting at the end — is one of the most commonly missing pieces.

It still counts if you:

  • Share sketches or a CAD screen over a call or message thread
  • Screenshot the exchange and add your own commentary beside it
  • Ask a parent or adult to give honest, critical feedback if a client truly isn’t reachable

Part 4

From Design to Reality

Communicating the design · manufacturing · making · testing

Stage 7

Communicating the final design

Enough detail that someone else could make this without asking you a single question.

Before you open each one, ask: what job does this drawing do?

  • How the finished piece actually looks from a useful angle.

  • How every part relates to and fits with every other part.

  • Real measurements — nothing left for guesswork.

  • A digital version that can be checked, shared and refined before you cut anything.

Stage 8

The manufacturing specification

Your plan for making it — step by step, written before you touch a machine.

One step from a real manufacturing specification

Column 1 of 5

  1. 1 Step & process

    Cut backrest curve from laminated blank

  2. 2 Tools & equipment

    Bandsaw, template, sanding drum

  3. 3 Health & safety

    Push stick; guard set to blade height; goggles

  4. 4 Quality check / tolerance

    ± 1mm to template; check by eye and by overlay

  5. 5 Reflection: what would I change?

    Sand to a finer grade before the next glue-up — some fibres tore

Every step gets all five — a rushed manufacturing plan (thin H&S, no tolerances, no reflection) is one of the most common reasons a strong design loses marks at this stage.

Stages 9–11

Make, test, evaluate — then close the loop

  1. Stage 9

    Make

    Follow your manufacturing spec; note where reality differed from the plan.

  2. Stage 10

    Test

    With your client, in the real environment the product is meant for.

  3. Stage 11

    Evaluate

    Against the original specification — point by point, not a general impression.

  4. Then

    Modify

    Propose specific changes — and note what they’d mean for the spec itself.

Modify → updated specification

Modifications feed back into an updated specification — the same document you started with, now shaped by everything you’ve learned.
