Primary research

What not to do

A real client interview, recorded during a live school project. Read what the client actually hands you.

The client designs it for you

Ask for opinions and you get solutions.

Interview transcript

Boarding House lamp brief. Verbatim, unedited.

What would be your budget/price range for the lamps?
It would be between $25-$30 for about 70 lamps seeing as I’m buying in batch for the Boarding House. But if they were for the communal area I’d pay around $50-$60 because I’d be buying less, maybe 3 or 4.
Any design movements you would like to be incorporated in the product?
I personally enjoy the style of Art Deco or semi Art Deco. I like minimal design that is not overly complicated but looks attractive and does its job.
Any specific type of materials would you like the lamp to have?
I prefer the use of wood as it can be attractive when lacquered or oiled and I don’t mind steel or iron. Wood works well because it fits in with the existing furniture in the Boarding house.
Is there any other functions you’d like the lamp to have?
If it was a bedside lamp, a lamp that had several uses, like a book stand, would be very useful as the space is quite small.
Is there any specific colours or themes you like to be involved in the lamp?
House colours are gold/yellow and black which would be nice if involved in the design.
Are there any specific safety features you need/want for the lamp?
Not being able to touch the bulb (if it emits heat) or the electrics, and it needs to not be able to break easily if knocked or dropped.

What not to do

Useful evidence, but the client has already designed the lamp.

  • Users suggest solutions. It is far more useful to know the cause behind the suggestion.
  • Early solutions close down creativity. Art Deco, wood, gold and black all arrive before anyone has looked at the room.
  • Strong design starts with problems, not products.

“A book stand” is an answer. “The space beside the bed is too small” is the problem worth designing for.

Research method

Poles Apart: reading preference without leading the witness

Show the user two contrasting images with no labels, and a bare scale between them. Ask only where their preference sits. Because there is nothing to agree with, the user cannot guess the answer you want, and you read aesthetic and lifestyle preference through a choice rather than an opinion.

Better questions

Building a questionnaire

Every question is an either / or. The user chooses; you infer.

User & Lifestyle

Helps define: size, storage, durability, flexibility.

Either / or prompts
  • Live alone or big family home?
  • Mostly relax or mostly work/study?
  • Entertain guests or rarely have visitors?
  • Move house or settled long-term?
  • Minimal or lots of belongings?
  • Tech-heavy or analogue/simple?
  • Indoor person or outdoor/adventurous?
  • Calm/quiet or busy/active home?
  • Formal/tidy or casual/relaxed?
  • Quick convenience or enjoy slow rituals?

Style & Aesthetics

Helps define: form, finish, materials, colour palette.

Either / or prompts
  • Modern/clean or rustic/natural?
  • Bold statement or blends in?
  • Light colours or dark tones?
  • Sharp lines or soft curves?
  • Minimal detail or decorative features?
  • Industrial look or warm/homey?
  • Timeless or trendy?
  • Show materials or painted finishes?
  • Symmetry or asymmetry?
  • Fun/playful or mature/sophisticated?

Function & Use

Helps define: mechanisms, ergonomics, dimensions.

Either / or prompts
  • Multi-purpose or dedicated function?
  • Lightweight or heavy/fixed?
  • Fold away or always on display?
  • Quick access or hidden storage?
  • Adjustable or fixed size?
  • Compact or spacious/oversized?
  • Comfort first or looks first?
  • Personal use or shared use?
  • Everyday use or occasional use?
  • Simple operation or feature-rich?

Sustainability & Values

Helps define: material choice, manufacturing method, lifecycle.

Either / or prompts
  • Natural materials or synthetic materials?
  • Buy once for life or cheaper/replaceable?
  • Locally made or imported?
  • Recycled materials or brand new?
  • Repairable or disposable?
  • Flat-pack efficient shipping or fully assembled?
  • Low environmental impact or lowest cost?
  • Visible eco-aesthetic or hidden sustainability?
  • Modular/upgradable or fixed design?
  • Handmade craft or mass-produced precision?

Cost & Practicality

Helps define: construction methods, fixings, specification level.

Either / or prompts
  • Premium quality or budget friendly?
  • Invest now or save money upfront?
  • Easy maintenance or luxury finish?
  • DIY assembly or professional install?
  • Long build time or fast delivery?
  • Custom made or off-the-shelf?
  • Robust/over-engineered or lightweight economy?
  • Replace parts or replace whole product?
  • Warranty important or not bothered?
  • Spend on durability or spend on style?

Designing the research

Now design your own method

Capturing consumer data is itself a design problem. Apply a mini version of the design process to how you gather the information, not just to the chair.

What information do you still need?

Look back at your five questionnaire categories — where are the gaps?

Find your gaps
  • User & Lifestyle
  • Style & Aesthetics
  • Function & Use
  • Sustainability & Values
  • Cost & Practicality

Pick the two or three you know least about your consumer.

What collection method will you use, and why?

Choose a method that fits the gap, not just the one that’s easiest.

  • Interview
  • Questionnaire / survey
  • Poles Apart scale
  • Observation
  • Mood-board reaction
  • Focus group
  • Existing-product trial
What each method is good for
  • Interview — best for causes and context, weak for large numbers.
  • Questionnaire — best for comparing many people quickly, weak for depth.
  • Poles Apart scale — best for style and lifestyle preference without leading questions.
  • Observation — best for real behaviour, weak for reasons why.
  • Mood-board reaction — best for aesthetic direction.
  • Focus group — best for discussion and disagreement between users.
  • Existing-product trial — best for spotting real frustrations with what is already out there.

What question types will you use, and why?

Each question type reveals something different. Match the type to what you need.

Question types and what they reveal
  • Open — reveals causes and context
  • Closed — reveals quick comparable facts
  • Scaled / Likert — reveals strength of preference
  • Ranking — reveals priority between options
  • Image-based / projective — reveals aesthetic and lifestyle preference without leading wording
  • Forced-choice — removes “don’t know” answers
  • Scenario-based — reveals behaviour in a specific situation

Before you run it, check:

  • Are your questions open enough to reveal causes, not solutions?
  • Have you avoided wording that leads to one answer?
  • Who exactly will you ask, and how many?
  • How will you record and keep the answers?
  • Will the answers actually convert into a specification line?
  • Have you planned to trial it on one person first and refine it?

A good method is designed, tested and improved — just like the product will be.
