The Idea Wall

This is the page pinned above the workbench: every idea gets on the wall, however it's made, and every idea says where it came from. Feasibility gets its say later, at the development stage — not now.

01  /  Starting point

Know your starting point

Every idea on this wall has to come from somewhere. Before you draw a single line, know where yours is coming from.

Every sketch on your sheet should point back to something you already know: a line from your design specification, something a client said, or an existing product you're modifying. Click a source below to see how it reads.

02  /  Mindset

Don't self-censor yet

"I won't draw that because I can't make it" is a development-stage sentence, not an initial-ideas one.
Showing: the trap
What kills a sheet of ideas
  • "I'm not sketching a fabric seat — sewing isn't my strong subject."
  • "I won't draw the floating version, I don't know how it would work yet."
  • "There's no point sketching six — I already know which one I'll pick."
What to write instead
  • Sketch the fabric seat. Label it "construction method TBC." Test it against your spec later, not now.
  • Sketch the floating version. Label it "mechanism to research." A half-solved idea can still be scored on its merits.
  • Draw all six. The comparison between them is what makes your final choice defensible on paper.
03  /  Mindset

Break your own assumptions

Every product type carries assumptions nobody wrote down. Find them, then design around their absence.
Why does a chair need four legs?

It doesn't — a chair needs to hold a person off the floor. Four legs is just the first answer, not the only one. Rock, hang, balance on one point, lean against a wall — each is a different structural idea worth its own sketch.

04  /  Communication

Annotate everything

A sketch shows the form. Notes show the thinking.

Every leader line should earn its place. Point at the thing you're actually explaining, not just the nearest edge.

  • Materials & possible manufacture ("could be made from…")
  • Function of a specific part or feature
  • Where the idea is inspired from (nature, an existing product, a client comment)
  • Which spec point it answers

Write it like you won't be there to explain it. An examiner or client reads the sheet cold — the annotation is doing all the talking.

  • Short phrases beat full sentences
  • Arrow to the exact detail, not the whole sketch
  • Note the "why," not just the "what"
05  /  Communication

Sketch in 3D

A flat outline can't show curvature, thickness, or how a form sits in space. An isometric or perspective sketch can.

Start with a box. "Crate" the rough volume in isometric first, then carve the real form out of it — it's faster than it sounds and keeps proportions honest.

  • Isometric: verticals stay vertical, the two base angles sit at 30°
  • Perspective: pick one or two vanishing points if the form is more organic
  • Rotate the same idea in a second, smaller sketch to check it from another angle
06  /  Range

Different, not more

Six sketches of the same shape from six angles isn't six ideas. Change what's actually different.

Before you sketch idea four, five and six, check idea one, two and three against these — if they all share the same answer, change the lens.
