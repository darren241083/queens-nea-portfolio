NEA ProblemFinding

“A strong NEA does not start with a product. It starts with a real person experiencing friction in a real context.”

Start with real people, real contexts and friction worth investigating. Product decisions come later.

Starting point

Find the friction first

Products come later

Notice friction before choosing a product.

Weak start

“I’m going to make a guitar stand.”
Product first

Stronger start

“A pupil carrying a guitar to school experiences transport and organisation friction when moving between home, lessons and rehearsals.”
User + friction first

“A product idea can wait until the user, context and evidence are clearer.”

NEA thinking

NEA thinking is a loop, not a straight line

Sketch the messy route from noticing to opportunity.

Problem finding is deliberately messy: pupils test what they notice against users, context and evidence before making product decisions.

Notice Question Gather evidence Make sense Reframe Spot opportunity

“Explore friction before product decisions. Pupils can move forward, but they should keep returning to the user, the context and the evidence.”

Friction-finding prompts

Five prompts before ideas

Mind-map starter: words that can help you begin exploring.

People

Who is actually involved?

Specific users

Be specific. Avoid “people who like music”. Use “a Year 8 pupil carrying a guitar to school” or “a music teacher setting up a lunchtime club”.

Places

Where does the activity happen?

Place examples

Classroom, rehearsal space, bus, car boot, school corridor, outdoor route, campsite, home, workshop.

Friction

What gets in the way?

Friction prompts

What is awkward, unsafe, uncomfortable, messy, slow, wasteful, expensive, difficult to store or difficult to clean?

Existing products

What already exists?

Product assumptions

What works well? What does not? What assumptions are built into current products?

Opportunities

Journey mapping

Before / During / After

“Useful friction often becomes visible when we map what a user does before, during and after an activity.”

Context: Exploring outdoors

Each stage can reveal friction.

Before

What happens before the activity starts?

Outdoor example

Look for friction while the user prepares, packs and makes decisions.

  • Packing a bag
  • Checking the weather
  • Loading the car
  • Choosing a route

During

What interrupts the activity?

Outdoor example

Watch for moments where the user has to stop, adapt or put up with something awkward.

  • Carrying items
  • Needing water or snacks
  • Keeping children safe
  • Finding somewhere clean to rest

After

What friction continues afterwards?

Outdoor example

Good opportunities often appear after the main activity has finished.

  • Cleaning muddy kit
  • Drying wet clothing
  • Storing equipment
  • Finding lost items

“The designer’s job is to notice friction: effort, risk, awkwardness or frustration.”

Friction trigger words

Words that reveal friction

Physical friction

  • Too heavy
  • Too awkward
  • Too uncomfortable

Emotional friction

  • Too stressful
  • Too easy to lose
  • Doesn’t suit the user

Context / environment friction

  • Too messy
  • Too hard to store
  • Too hard to clean
  • Doesn’t fit the space

Access / safety friction

  • Too unsafe
  • Not accessible enough
  • Too expensive

Social friction

  • Too slow
  • Too much waste
  • Hard to share
How pupils use these words

Put a trigger word next to a real user and a real place. Then ask: when does this happen, why does it matter, and what evidence could prove it?

How to use the ingredients

Use these as prompts to connect a specific user, an action or need, the friction they experience, and the context or evidence that makes the opportunity worth exploring.

These are possible starting points for discussion, not finished NEA statements.

Music example

A possible user, friction and context.

Show ingredient set
User
A young musician travelling between home, school and rehearsals.
Friction
Sheet music, headphones and small accessories are easy to forget or damage.
Evidence / context
Movement between lessons, rehearsals and home routines.
Opportunity direction
Explore easier ways to organise and protect small music items.

Classroom example

A named school context with clear friction.

Show ingredient set
User
A Year 10 DT pupil working in a practical lesson.
Friction
Tools, materials and an iPad create clutter on limited desk space.
Evidence / context
Moving between designing, making, recording and packing away.
Opportunity direction
Explore clearer organisation for shared lesson workspaces.

Outdoor example

A common activity explored as a possible design opportunity.

Show ingredient set
User
A family walker returning from poor weather.
Friction
Muddy or wet outdoor kit can make the car or home messy.
Evidence / context
After-walk routines, storage, cleaning and transport.
Opportunity direction
Explore easier ways to separate, dry, clean or store kit.

GCSE contexts

GCSE contextual challenge examples

Making or enjoying music

People Friction Opportunities

Look at carrying, storing, setting up and protecting music kit.

Possible users

Music pupils, performers, teachers, buskers, pupils carrying instruments.

Possible frictions

Full-size instruments can be awkward to carry, store or use, especially for younger pupils or pupils travelling through busy school spaces. Large instruments may create transport, storage, safety or access friction.

Possible opportunities

Evidence might point towards lighter, compact or easier-to-carry responses, depending on what user research shows.

In the classroom

People Friction Opportunities

Watch how people work, move, store things and share space.

Possible users

Pupils, teachers, technicians, SEND pupils, visitors.

Possible frictions

Desk clutter, storage, posture, device use, tool organisation, quick packing away, accessibility.

Possible opportunities

Evidence could point towards clearer desk organisation, adaptable storage, better access to tools or easier movement between designing and making.

Exploring outdoors

People Friction Opportunities

Map what happens before, during and after the activity.

Possible users

Walkers, families, cyclists, campers, school groups, dog walkers.

Possible frictions

Carrying kit, wet or muddy equipment, safety, resting, weather protection, storage, sustainability.

Possible opportunities

User evidence could suggest easier ways to carry, separate, dry, clean or store outdoor kit before, during or after an activity.

A-level depth check

Can the context sustain investigation, modelling and testing?

Depth checklist
  • Real user or client
  • Genuine user need or friction
  • Clear constraints
  • Primary research potential
  • Technical challenge
  • Scope for modelling and testing
  • Materials and process depth
  • Sustainability or lifecycle considerations
  • Evaluation with real users
Warning sign

“A simple product type only has depth if the user evidence, uncertainty, technical challenge, modelling and testing are strong enough.”

Final teaching point

“Today is not about choosing a final product. It is about finding a worthwhile design opportunity.”
